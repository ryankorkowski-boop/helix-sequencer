## What the three logic passes changed

**Detection and routing.** Retained source hashes and confidence screening; broadened the mallet register and source-screened ringing candidates without accepting voice/noise leakage or inventing notes. Piano and mallet provenance remain separate. Drum classification scores, harmonic-contamination/ambiguity gates and approved guitar event extraction are preserved rather than globally lowering thresholds. No learned model was retrained. Neutral avatars and explicit voice intervals avoid unsupported gender/duet guesses.

**Timing and motion.** Shorter bass strokes, anticipatory key travel with protected source contact frames, exact depressed-key/hand agreement, exact bar hits, connected inverse-kinematic limbs and realistic drummer grip/rebound. The final keyboard audit found and fixed a real overlapping-transition defect. Sorted EventLane indices replace repeated whole-event scans for drummer and mallet neighbors, with stable duplicate-time/random-access behavior. The technical gallery’s tiny display markers use four faces instead of20 per LED, preserving all existing review-node positions and lighting slots while reducing marker geometry; performer meshes are unchanged. A measured 5,000-event / 5,000-query benchmark fell from 0.522 seconds to 0.00209 seconds, approximately 249.5× faster for that lookup only. It does not mean Helix or rendering overall is 249.5× faster.

**Failure handling and exports.** The direct engine entry previously printed song failures yet could return as though generation succeeded. A five-line guard continues independent songs and then raises a chained failure identifying failed inputs. Detection/scoring algorithms are untouched. Existing Android extended-MP4, numerical parity, cancellation and preview-failure recovery tests remain. Provenance is added without changing musical channel values, LOR addresses or input templates. UTF-8 BOM/declaration preservation is tested. Incomplete/EOF-sized MP4s are rejected rather than delivered; the final garden was rerendered after that validation caught an incomplete container.

## Commercial potential

**Recommended positioning:** sell Helix first as an audio-to-show authoring assistant that reduces sequencing time and supplies editable, repeatable geometry/motion. Approach stadium integrators through paid preproduction pilots. Keep the existing MIT core free; earn revenue from installation work, support, hosted computation, original separately licensed premium assets and future proprietary additions whose ownership is established.

The price ranges below are **proposed test offers in USD, not observed Helix sales, a company valuation, market-size research or guaranteed demand**. They exclude fixtures/controllers, music clearance, travel/taxes and operator staffing unless expressly contracted.

Professional software benchmarks are far below a complete stadium integration contract: Capture lists €395/€795/€1,295/€2,195 for Solo/Duet/Quartet/Symphony new licenses [1]; TouchDesigner lists $600 Commercial and $2,200 Pro [2]. MA offers its grandMA3 software free, while onPC DMX output uses its hardware [3]. These examples support a service/integration-led offer. They do not demonstrate willingness to pay Helix’s proposed enterprise prices, and their license models cannot be imposed on Helix’s existing MIT grants.

An illustrative customer ROI test: at an assumed $100/hour loaded programming cost, saving 30 hours on each of eight productions yields $24,000/year gross saved time. A $10,000 service could be worthwhile before integration/support costs; a $35,000 service would require more value or greater use. Measure real before/after hours, revision time and acceptance on paid pilots. These numbers are assumptions, not measured Helix performance.

A responsible stadium pilot should prove deterministic playback/timecode, operator overrides/blackout, fixture/color patch accuracy, recovery/failover and resource scaling on an actual installation. Established consoles/media servers should retain live output authority. A future sACN adapter would need current protocol testing; ESTA lists ANSI E1.31-2025 for network transport of DMX data [4]. That adapter, compliance and operational reliability have not been implemented or certified here.

## Licensing, music and attribution

The repository is currently MIT-licensed, with commercial use/distribution permissions and required notices. The legal pages preserve that grant. A compulsory annual vendor fee or mandatory public output credit cannot be retroactively added to the existing MIT core merely by putting it in a Legal tab. Separate services and future separately licensed original assets can have written annual/attribution terms after rights review. The watermark supplies generator credit and hashes; it is not DRM, proof of copyright or an enforcement mechanism.

The pages identify demo music as supplied by Helix / Ryan Korkowski, but do not assert that every song is exclusively owned or freely redistributable. Track creation account, creation date, subscription entitlement and remix/third-party conditions still need a track-specific rights ledger. Current Suno terms distinguish paid output assignment, free-tier personal/noncommercial use and remix limitations [6]; the applicable terms at each track’s creation must also be checked. The U.S. Copyright Office says AI outputs require sufficient human-authored expressive elements for copyright; prompts alone do not suffice [7]. The new previews are for your review and are not a public music-rights clearance or release.
